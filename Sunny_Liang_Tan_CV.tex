\section*{Job Market Paper}
\paper{Creating or Correcting Misallocation? Government Venture Capital and Network Propagation in China}
Abstract: Does industrial policy correct misallocation or create new distortions? We examine government venture capital in China by quantifying its aggregate TFP implications, separating technological gains from improvements in resource allocation. To estimate the aggregate TFP effect, we need to establish where resources are directed, how firms' technology and input use respond, and what output is forgone elsewhere. Using linked investment, administrative, patent, and supplier--buyer data, we measure baseline marginal products and estimate recipient and trading-partner responses with a matched staggered difference-in-differences design. Production-network sufficient statistics then value the estimated resource transfers at marginal-product gaps relative to firms releasing resources and add the technological contribution. We find that recipients are concentrated in strategic sectors, and both recipients and their trading partners exhibit greater innovative activity. Further, recipients and suppliers have higher pre-treatment marginal revenue products of capital and labor than the same-industry firms supplying the offsetting resources, whereas buyers have lower marginal products. Capital and employment subsequently expand across all three groups, with positive allocative contributions from recipients and suppliers and a negative contribution from buyers. Firm-level physical productivity (TFPQ) shows no detectable short-run increase. Taken together, these responses imply a manufacturing TFP-equivalent gain of approximately $0.07\%$ under fixed industry factor supplies, driven principally by recipients. At the scale of 2016 manufacturing, the gain is equivalent to approximately RMB~14.51 billion in additional output, before incremental policy implementation costs. The findings suggest that public capital can improve allocation when private venture finance is underdeveloped and investment selection retains commercial incentives.
\section*{Working Paper}
\textbf{Exchange-Rate Movements and U.S. Corporations} with Ivo Welch and Yuqing Zhou\par\nopagebreak

Abstract: This paper revisits the exchange-rate exposure puzzle: why prior research finds weak relationships between currency movements and U.S. firms' stock returns despite substantial international trade. Linking transaction-level U.S. Census export records to financial statements and stock returns for publicly traded U.S. exporters from 1992 to 2016, we construct firm-specific exchange-rate exposure measures that reflect each firm's export destinations. Dollar depreciation is associated with economically significant increases in exports, sales, profitability, and stock returns, with export, sales, and stock-return sensitivities generally larger among larger and more export-oriented firms. In asset- or export-weighted specifications, a 1\% depreciation is associated with approximately 0.4-0.6\% higher exports and 0.6-0.8\% higher sales. The stronger sales response is consistent with currency movements affecting domestic competitiveness as well as foreign demand, while the profitability results indicate that financial and operational hedging do not fully offset exposure. Real operating sensitivities increase over time. Stock prices respond contemporaneously, with no detectable delayed response around subsequent earnings announcements. By measuring exposure using firms' actual trading relationships and tracing its implications across operating outcomes and equity valuations, the paper contributes new evidence to the exchange-rate exposure literature and shows that substantial corporate currency exposure can coexist with timely incorporation of information into stock prices.
\section*{Work in Progress}
\textbf{Industrial Policy and Scale Complementarities in EV Battery Supply Chains} with Junjie Xia and Binkai Chen\par\nopagebreak

Abstract: This project studies whether increasing returns at successive stages of production amplify industrial subsidies through mutually reinforcing demand and cost linkages. Support for EV battery producers may expand demand for battery materials, allowing upstream suppliers to achieve lower unit costs. If these savings are passed through to battery producers, they can induce further downstream expansion. We have linked income-tax balance-sheet records, VAT data, and firm-to-firm transactions for Anhui province. The planned analysis traces policy-induced expansion through preexisting supply relationships and examines suppliers' scale, costs, investment, and sales to other buyers. Where product quantities and prices can be measured consistently, we will investigate the transmission of upstream cost savings to downstream customers. We aim to distinguish scale economies from changes in demand, markups, product composition, and supplier selection, and to identify which benefits decentralized firms fail to internalize. The quantitative goal is to discipline a production-network model with these estimates and compare upstream, downstream, and joint support under a common fiscal budget.
\par\textbf{Industrial Policy and Induced Innovation in Semiconductor Supply Chains} with Meryvn Ho\par\nopagebreak
Abstract: This paper studies whether industrial policy induces technological upgrading among semiconductor equipment makers by supporting their downstream customers. Chipmakers' adoption of more advanced production technologies raises the technical requirements facing equipment suppliers. The resulting demand can make costly equipment development worthwhile, while repeated production, testing, and customer feedback can generate learning that benefits subsequent customers. Combining government venture capital investment records, administrative firm data, and supplier-buyer transactions with semiconductor fabrication capacity and process-technology data, the project traces equipment makers' exposure to policy-supported chipmakers through pre-existing supply relationships. The empirical analysis distinguishes customer capacity expansion from advances in process technology and examines whether these changes induce corresponding capability upgrading among equipment suppliers. It investigates whether supplier upgrading varies with the duration and intensity of exposure to technologically advancing customers, and whether newly developed capabilities extend to other buyers. Changes in sourcing relationships help distinguish upgrading within incumbent suppliers from substitution toward suppliers that already possess advanced capabilities. The economic mechanism is a dynamic complementarity between downstream technological adoption and upstream innovation: customer upgrading creates incentives for equipment development, while improvements in equipment enable further adoption. The paper aims to identify when industrial policy activates this process, generating transferable learning and technological advancement beyond directly financed firms.
